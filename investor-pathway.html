2. The Three Paths — Plain English

Think of them as three different doors. One can lead to owning property, one starts with a lien claim, and one may let you buy something the regular auction did not sell.

Path 1

Tax Deed / Tax-Sale Property

You are bidding to buy the property through a government or court-supervised sale. You are trying to buy the house or land itself—not simply earn interest on a tax bill.

Goal: property ownershipNeeds: auction funds + due diligence
Path 2

Tax Lien Certificate

You pay delinquent taxes and receive a legal claim called a certificate. You do not own the house just because you bought the certificate. The owner may pay it back with interest.

Goal: possible interest incomeNeeds: state-specific rules + patience
Path 3

Over-the-Counter (OTC)

After an auction, some places let investors buy certain unsold certificates or properties directly from the county. “OTC” describes how it is offered—not one universal type of investment.

Goal: find post-auction inventoryNeeds: confirm what is being sold
PATH 1 — Tax deed / tax-sale property: how it works

In simple words: The government has a legal process for collecting unpaid property taxes. In a tax-deed state, the property can eventually be offered for sale. An investor bids on the property itself.

Typical steps (the details differ by state)

  1. Find the official tax-sale list and read the sale rules.
  2. Research the parcel, property condition, title, liens, access, occupancy and any redemption rights.
  3. Register and arrange money that will be available by the sale deadlines.
  4. Bid only up to a maximum price worked out before the auction.
  5. If you win, pay the required deposit and balance on time.
  6. Complete the deed, title and possession steps required by that jurisdiction.
Sample: A county advertises a small house for a tax-deed auction. An investor researches the title and estimates repairs. The investor sets a $70,000 maximum bid. The winning bid is $62,000. The investor must still pay the deposit and remaining balance within the county's deadline, plus applicable fees and later repair/ownership costs. Winning the bid does not mean the house is ready to live in or rent.

What can go wrong?

The building may be damaged or occupied. Some liens, title defects, access problems or court steps may remain. A sale may have redemption or challenge rights. You may need a lawyer, title professional, contractor and extra cash. Never assume the auction price is the full cost.

PATH 2 — Tax lien certificate: how it works

In simple words: You are buying a claim for unpaid taxes—not buying the home. The property owner may pay the tax debt to clear the claim. If that happens, the investor receives the amount due under the state's rules, which may include interest and costs.

Typical steps in a certificate state

  1. Check whether that state actually sells tax lien certificates to private investors.
  2. Review the parcel and all rules before bidding or buying.
  3. Purchase the certificate and track its deadlines.
  4. If the owner redeems, collect the amount paid under the statute.
  5. If the owner does not redeem, you may be able to start a separate legal tax-deed process after the required waiting period.
  6. That next process can require more money, notices, searches, fees and possibly another auction. Ownership is not automatic.
Sample interest outcome: Suppose an investor buys a $4,000 certificate in a state where the applicable example rate is 12% per year. If it is redeemed after one year and the rules allow that full calculation, the simple-interest illustration is $480 before costs and taxes. The investor receives the certificate amount plus the amount legally due. This is an illustration—not a promised rate or return.
Important: If the owner does not pay, do not change the story to “the investor gets the house.” The investor must follow every notice, deadline and tax-deed / foreclosure step, pay required additional amounts, and may still have to compete at a property auction.
PATH 3 — Over-the-counter (OTC): how it works

In simple words: A tax item was offered at the regular auction but did not sell, or it was retained by the county. In places that allow it, the county may offer it directly afterward. There may be no live bidding war, but the county's price, fees and eligibility rules still apply.

There are two different things people call “OTC”

  • OTC lien certificate: You buy an unsold or county-held tax certificate. It is still a lien claim, not a house. Redemption and any later tax-deed process follow the state's law.
  • OTC / repository property: You make an offer on an unsold property held by a county after an earlier sale. If approved and paid for, the county may convey the property by deed, subject to that county's process.
Sample certificate: A county's auction ends with a $3,500 tax certificate still held by the county. The county's rules allow an investor to purchase it later. The investor pays the amount the county requires. The investor now owns the certificate—not the house. The owner may still redeem it, or the investor may later qualify to begin a tax-deed process.
Sample repository property: A Pennsylvania property remains unsold after a judicial tax sale. The county places it on a repository list and may accept a qualified offer under its rules. If the offer is accepted and the buyer pays, the county arranges a deed. That is an OTC property purchase, not a tax-lien certificate investment.

3. What this means in Pennsylvania

This is the local warning for a Pennsylvania investor: do not copy another state's tax-lien playbook and assume it works here.

Pennsylvania county repository sales: A property that remains unsold after a judicial tax sale may be placed in a county Tax Claim Bureau's repository for unsold properties. The county may accept bids subject to its rules and required taxing-district consent. In Berks County, the official repository page explains this process and currently says bidding reopens March 1, 2027. That is one county's schedule—not a statewide date.
Philadelphia Sheriff tax sales: Philadelphia lists tax-delinquent, tax-collection and tax-lien auctions. These are property sale proceedings, and bidders must follow the specific sale terms. The current posted terms describe a 10% deposit requirement, with the balance of the purchase price due within 15 calendar days (subject to the stated weekend/holiday rule). Read the exact listing and sale terms before bidding.
Do not mix these up: A Philadelphia auction labelled “Tax Lien” is not automatically the same as buying a Florida-style tax lien certificate for interest. Philadelphia's Sheriff FAQ distinguishes its tax-delinquent and tax-lien sale categories; review the actual parcel listing and legal sale documents.

A Pennsylvania state tax lien filed by the Department of Revenue is also not, by itself, an investor certificate for sale. It is a recorded claim securing unpaid Commonwealth taxes.

4. Example: how the profile points someone to a lane

Made-up example for learning—not a real client, property, offer or financing approval.

Investor profile: New investor, wants to eventually own a rental home in Pennsylvania, has around $12,000 available, has no property yet, may have a contractor partner, and is still learning.

What the profile should say: “Start with education and carefully verified Pennsylvania repository / tax-sale opportunities. Do not assume $12,000 is enough to win and settle a Philadelphia Sheriff sale. Explore a properly documented partnership or other capital source only after the exact auction payment deadlines and all-in costs are known.”

Why? Philadelphia's published terms require substantial payment shortly after a winning bid and the remaining balance within the sale deadline. A future loan, uncommitted partner or expected refinance is not cash in hand. Read the official sale terms.

5. Questions to ask the course provider when you enroll

  1. Can you review my investor profile and tell me which strategy fits my state, cash level and goal?
  2. Do you teach tax deed auctions, tax lien certificates and OTC purchases as three separate strategies?
  3. Which states and counties do you actively support, and what official county pages should I use?
  4. When you say “OTC,” do you mean an unsold lien certificate or an unsold property / repository listing?
  5. Can a beginner become a property owner through this path, and what extra legal steps and costs come before ownership?
  6. What funds must be available immediately, and which funding sources are accepted by the auction authority?
  7. Can you show a completed example from list research through payment, deed and final ownership—or, for a lien, through redemption or tax-deed application?
  8. Do you offer a one-on-one review after the profile is completed, and what is included in the full upgrade?
